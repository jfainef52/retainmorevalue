"You deferred the tax. You did not eliminate it. It has been growing alongside the balance the entire time."
The statement shows one number. Your family receives a very different one.
Every other asset you own is taxed once, or receives a step-up in basis and escapes tax entirely. The qualified plan does neither.
🏛️
It Is Counted in Your Estate
The full balance is included in your gross estate at death. Above the federal exemption, that exposure is taxed at 40% — and the bill is due in cash within nine months.
💵
Then Taxed Again as Income
Whatever remains is ordinary income to whoever inherits it, at their rates, not yours. There is no step-up in basis. Under current law the entire balance must come out within ten years.
⏰
And You Cannot Simply Leave It Alone
Required distributions begin whether or not you need the income. The tax is paid, and what is left lands right back in your estate to be taxed a second time at death.

The Structure
How
It
Works
FOUR STEPS,
NOTHING OUT
OF POCKET
"The full pre-tax balance goes to work — not what is left after writing a check to the IRS."

Your qualified plan funds a life insurance asset held outside your estate, and the tax on the distribution is financed rather than paid from your assets.

1
Distribute on a Schedule You Choose
The plan is distributed over a defined funding period rather than left to be settled at death under the worst available tax treatment.
2
Finance the Tax Obligation
A commercial lender finances the income tax due, secured by the policy rather than your other assets. Nothing additional comes out of your pocket.
3
Fund the Policy Inside a Trust
An irrevocable trust owns the policy from inception, so the death benefit is never part of your taxable estate. Your attorney drafts and administers the trust.
4
Retire the Financing from the Benefit
The lender is retired from within the policy, and the balance passes to your family free of income tax and outside your estate.
⚖️
The trust is not optional
Held personally, the death benefit is free of income tax but still counted in your estate. Held in a properly structured irrevocable trust, it is outside the estate entirely. That single distinction is worth a material share of the result.

Method
How We
Modeled
It
FIFTY YEARS
OF ACTUAL
MARKET
HISTORY
"We did not pick a rate of return and compound it. We tested the plan against every market this country has actually had."

A probability distribution built from fifty years of real market history — applied to your qualified plan.

1
Real Years, Drawn at Random
Ten thousand scenarios are built by drawing actual paired stock and bond years from 1975 through 2024 — keeping equity and bond results together as they actually occurred, rather than assuming a smooth average.
2
Required Distributions Applied
Each scenario applies required minimum distributions on the IRS schedule at your age, stacks them on top of taxable Social Security, and reinvests what is left in a taxable account — because you do not need the income. Medicare premium surcharges triggered by that income are counted as well.
3
The Median, Not the Best Case
Every figure shown is the median — the middle outcome of ten thousand. Half the scenarios finish better and half finish worse.
4
The Insurance Side Is Not Modeled
Policy values are taken directly from the carrier's own illustration as prepared. Nothing on that side of the comparison is projected by us.
⚖️
Where the assumptions favour leaving things as they are
Two choices in this analysis deliberately run against the recommendation. Required distributions on the do-nothing path are taxed as though they were your only ordinary income, so they fill the lowest brackets and produce the smallest possible tax on that path. The distribution funding the alternative is taxed at the highest bracket. Any error in either direction understates the advantage shown, never overstates it.
📏
Why the median is the fair comparison
The median outcome for your plan implies roughly the same annual return the carrier used in its illustration. Comparing the two at that level is a like-for-like comparison. Figures are hypothetical and are not a guarantee of future results.
